# Dynamic Stall CFD and Plasma Actuator Flow Control

## Overview
This project investigates the unsteady aerodynamic phenomenon of **dynamic stall** on a **pitching NACA0012 airfoil** using CFD, and further explores the use of **plasma actuators** as an active flow-control method to delay separation and modify vortex shedding behaviour.

Dynamic stall occurs when an airfoil pitches rapidly through large angles of attack, causing flow separation to be delayed beyond the static stall angle. This results in the formation of a strong **dynamic stall vortex (DSV)** near the leading edge, which temporarily increases lift before a sudden breakdown in aerodynamic performance. Repeated dynamic stall cycles are particularly relevant in applications such as **helicopter rotors** and **wind turbine blades**, where the effective inflow conditions vary continuously with time.

The project was carried out in two stages:

- baseline CFD simulation of dynamic stall on a pitching airfoil  
- implementation and evaluation of plasma actuators for dynamic stall control  

The main objective was to quantify:

- lift hysteresis during pitching motion  
- formation and shedding of the dynamic stall vortex  
- influence of plasma actuator placement on separation delay and reattachment  

---

# Numerical Method

## Governing Approach
The flow was simulated using **k-ω SST URANS** for a pitching **NACA0012 airfoil** undergoing prescribed sinusoidal motion.

The pitching kinematics were defined as:

<p><em>α(t) = 15° + 10° sin(ωt)</em></p>

with a **reduced frequency** of:

<p><em>k = 0.15</em></p>

This case was selected as a baseline because relevant experimental reference data from NASA reports is available for comparison.

The objective of the baseline simulation was to capture the key features of dynamic stall:

- delayed separation during pitch-up  
- formation of the dynamic stall vortex  
- lift overshoot  
- strong hysteresis during the pitch cycle  

---

## Solver Implementation
The mesh was generated around the NACA0012 airfoil with an initial **15° tilt**, allowing the pitching motion to be imposed in Fluent using a **`DEFINE_CG_MOTION` UDF**.

The computational domain was divided into two zones:

- an outer **C-grid** region for the far-field  
- an inner circular **O-grid** region surrounding the airfoil, where the rotational motion was applied  

This setup allowed the airfoil to pitch dynamically while maintaining mesh quality around the body.

## Plasma Actuator Modelling
To model active flow control, a simplified **Suzen–Huang plasma actuator model** was implemented using **User-Defined Scalars (UDS)** in ANSYS Fluent.

The approach introduces two scalar fields:

- an electric potential field  
- a net charge density field  

The electric field is obtained from the gradient of the potential:

<p><em>E = −∇ϕ</em></p>

Two scalar transport equations are solved:

- a Poisson-type equation for the electric potential  
- a diffusion-type equation for the charge density, including a decay term representing charge relaxation  

These equations are implemented through Fluent’s generic scalar transport formulation with appropriate diffusion coefficients and source terms.

The plasma actuator effect is introduced into the flow through a **body force term** added to the momentum equations:

<p><em>f<sub>b</sub> = ρ<sub>c</sub> E</em></p>

which, in component form, acts as a momentum source:

<p><em>f<sub>x</sub> = ρ<sub>c</sub> (−∂ϕ/∂x)</em></p>
<p><em>f<sub>y</sub> = ρ<sub>c</sub> (−∂ϕ/∂y)</em></p>

This body force represents the momentum addition generated by the plasma actuator, effectively energising the near-wall flow and modifying separation behaviour.

## Boundary Conditions for Plasma Actuator

The plasma actuator model requires boundary conditions for both the electric potential (<em>ϕ</em>) and the charge density (<em>ρ<sub>c</sub></em>) fields.

The following conditions were applied:

- **Exposed electrode (active electrode):**  
  <p><em>ϕ = 1, &nbsp;&nbsp; ∇ρ<sub>c</sub> · n = 0</em></p>  
  A fixed electric potential is imposed, while a zero-flux condition is applied to the charge density.

- **Encapsulated electrode (covered electrode):**  
  <p><em>ϕ = 0, &nbsp;&nbsp; ρ<sub>c</sub> = ρ<sub>c,max</sub> G(x)</em></p>  
  The electric potential is grounded, and a prescribed spatial distribution is applied to the charge density to represent the plasma generation region.

- **All other boundaries (airfoil surface and far-field):**  
  <p><em>∇ϕ · n = 0, &nbsp;&nbsp; ρ<sub>c</sub> = 0</em></p>  
  A zero-flux condition is applied for the electric potential, and no charge density is imposed.

These boundary conditions allow the formation of a localised electric field near the electrode region, generating a body force that interacts with the near-wall flow.

# Flow Physics

Dynamic stall differs from static stall because the airfoil motion delays the onset of separation, allowing the airfoil to reach much higher instantaneous angles of attack before the boundary layer detaches.

The main flow-physics features observed in this project include:

- delayed leading-edge separation during pitch-up  
- roll-up of a strong dynamic stall vortex  
- temporary lift enhancement due to the DSV  
- abrupt lift drop after vortex shedding  
- reattachment lag during the downstroke  

The role of the plasma actuator is to inject momentum into the near-wall flow and shear layer. However, the effectiveness of the control depends strongly on **actuator placement relative to the separation point**.

# Key Results

## Static Stall Verification
To verify the plasma actuator implementation, a static angle-of-attack sweep was first carried out.

For the static case, two plasma actuators were placed at **0.1c** and **0.3c**.

Electric field contour showing the location of the Plasma actuators in the static case.

![](/images/DS_phi_Static.png)

These positions had previously been identified as favourable for delaying static stall. The results showed a clear delay in stall with actuation, confirming that the plasma model was implemented correctly and that the actuator was imparting momentum effectively to the boundary layer.

![](/images/DS_Static.png)

---

## Baseline Dynamic Stall
The baseline unsteady simulation successfully captured the key characteristics of dynamic stall:

- lift hysteresis over the pitching cycle  
- development of the dynamic stall vortex  
- sharp changes in aerodynamic response during vortex shedding  

This provided the reference case for assessing the effect of plasma actuation.

---

## Plasma Actuator Effects in Dynamic Stall
When the same actuator locations used in the static case were applied to the dynamic stall problem, the behaviour changed significantly.

The actuator arrangement at **0.1c and 0.3c** was found to trigger **earlier shedding of the dynamic stall vortex**. Although this produced higher lift during the upward stroke, it also led to a more abrupt and highly dynamic downstroke response.

Lift hysteresis for static case Plasma actuator arrangement.

![](/images/DS_PA12.png)

Analysis of the vorticity field suggested that the actuators were largely located within the separated region. Instead of stabilising the shear layer, they appeared to destabilise it, causing earlier DSV shedding and preventing effective reattachment. A similar trend was observed even when only the upstream actuator was retained.

Vorticity contour showing the destabilising counter vorticity produced by the plasma actuators inside the separated region.

![](/images/DS_PA2_close.png)

---

## Leading-Edge Actuator Placement
To improve the control strategy, the plasma actuator was repositioned at the **leading edge** of the airfoil.

This placement was found to be more effective because momentum was introduced **before the separation point**, helping to energise the shear layer at the right location.

Electric field contour showing the Plasma actuator positioned at the Leading edge.

![](/images/DS_phi_LE.png)

With this configuration:

- the shedding of the DSV was slightly delayed  
- the lift hysteresis loop improved  
- earlier reattachment was observed during the downstroke  
- the duration over which strong dynamic stall effects dominated was reduced  

These results indicate that actuator placement is critical, and that a location effective for static stall does not necessarily remain effective under unsteady dynamic stall conditions.
